---
title: The Garden Behind the Tracks
---

For three years we were on the waiting list. Then in March the chairman of the allotment association rang and said that a garden behind the railway tracks had become free. We drove over that same afternoon. A narrow path runs between the gardens, with tall hedges to the left and right, and every few minutes a train goes past that you stop hearing altogether after a week.

The garden was in no good state. The previous owner is almost ninety and had not been able to come for the last few years. The grass stood high, the beds were full of nettles, and inside the hut it smelled of wet wood. We signed all the same, because we knew that otherwise we would have to wait years again.

An allotment is not a piece of meadow that you can simply buy. There are statutes, and those statutes are rather precise: a third of the plot has to be planted with fruit or vegetables, the hedge may be no higher than one metre twenty, and officially you are not allowed to spend the night in the hut. At the beginning I laughed at rules like that. By now I understand them better, because without them the colony would long since have turned into a row of small plots on which everyone keeps to themselves.

Our neighbour Mr Wolter has had his garden since 1987. During the first week he stood at the fence every day and watched us struggling with the scythe. On the Friday he came across the path with his lawnmower, without saying anything beforehand. Since then he brings us courgettes in the summer that nobody can eat any more, and we bring him cake in return. On the other side a family from Vietnam does their gardening; from them we have learned that you can plant herbs far closer together than the books say.

In the first year we did a great deal wrong. We put the tomatoes out too early, and in a cold week in May they died off. The old pear tree, on the other hand, bore so much fruit that we gave it away to everyone we know. When I ride through the gate on a Saturday morning nowadays, I rarely think about vegetables. I think rather about the fact that forty families share something here that in a city usually belongs to only a few: a piece of earth, a little time and a place where you have to ask nobody's permission if you simply want to sit.
