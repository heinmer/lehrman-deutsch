---
title: Measuring Silence
---

On an ordinary weekday morning, a man is standing at the kerb in Frankfurt's Nordend district with a tripod, holding a microphone up in the air as though he were waiting for a signal from outer space. What he is in fact measuring is what everyone can hear anyway: the traffic, the squeal of the tram on the bend, the rolling of suitcases over the cobblestones. Two hours later all of it will have been turned into a single number, into a decibel value that will be entered on a map, compared with legal limits and, should it turn out too high, become the subject of an administrative procedure. What is being recorded here is, strictly speaking, not the noise but its reverse side: silence, the one quantity in public life that is measured exclusively by what it lacks. Anyone who wants to protect it must first translate it into numbers, and it is at that very translation that opinions begin to divide.

That noise wears people down is no discovery of the present day. Schopenhauer devoted an essay of its own to the whip-cracking of carters and regarded sensitivity to noise as a reliable measure of intellectual ability — a claim that has earned him a good deal of mockery. In 1908 the philosopher Theodor Lessing founded an anti-noise society in Hanover whose members took up arms against carpet-beating, barking dogs and the brass bands of the excursion inns, and for a short while it looked as though the irritation of a few scholars might turn into a social movement. The society fell apart after a few years; what has remained is the observation that every age considers the noise of its own time louder than that of its predecessors. What is new, if anything, is that the dispute is no longer fought out between neighbours but between expert reports.

What tipped the scales was a change of perspective: for some decades now noise has counted not merely as a nuisance but as a health risk. The World Health Organization recommends levels for road traffic that are clearly exceeded in many city centres, and it bases those recommendations on studies according to which sustained exposure at night raises blood pressure and measurably increases the risk of cardiovascular disease. What matters is a finding that runs counter to common sense: the sleeping body responds to a passing freight train with stress hormones even when the sleeper insists the next morning that they heard nothing at all. Habituation, so the uncomfortable conclusion runs, takes place in the mind, not in the circulation. Since the European environmental noise directive, larger cities have been obliged to draw up noise maps every five years and to submit action plans; anyone who has ever seen such a map recognises their own city by the red arteries of its main roads.

Only, that map answers a different question from the one that troubles the people affected. The averaged sound level on which it rests spreads the acoustic energy of an entire night evenly across its duration, so that a single shunting yard and a permanently busy bypass can produce the same value, even though the one tears sleep apart while the other merely underlays it. There is also the fact that how annoying a sound is depends far less on its energy than on who is causing it and on whether one ever agreed to it. Church bells are tolerated, the concert in the back courtyard is celebrated and the neighbour's heat pump is taken to court, even though the measuring device can barely tell any appreciable difference between them. Attempts have been made to capture this in surcharges, for tones, for impulses, for the night-time hours, and yet the impression remains that something is here being recorded with great precision which is precisely what eludes precision.

Silence becomes politically explosive at the moment it grows scarce, for it is unevenly distributed. Flats on widened arterial roads are cheaper than those by the park, which is why noise exposure in almost every German city correlates reliably with the income of its residents — a connection that is rarely spelled out as plainly in the action plans as it emerges from the maps. The directive does at least provide for quiet areas to be designated and protected against any increase in noise; in practice that protection regularly collides with the equally legitimate wish to build more densely within growing cities rather than to build on further fields. Anyone who wants both at once ends up in a dilemma that cannot be planned away. Even the restructuring of transport brings only limited relief: a new tram line replaces hundreds of car journeys and nevertheless leads to objections from precisely those residents who have been demanding better public transport for years.

Perhaps the real mistake lies in thinking of silence as mere absence. When John Cage stepped into an anechoic chamber around 1950 in order to hear nothing at all for once, he perceived two sounds, one high and one low, and was told on enquiry that they had been his nervous system and his circulation; from then on he no longer spoke of absolute silence. What people are really asking for when they call for quiet is therefore seldom the zero on the scale, but the possibility of having their own hearing at their disposal and not having to listen to what others have decided for them. Acoustics as yet knows no unit for that; the authorities go on measuring in decibels because they cannot measure in anything else, and the maps grow more accurate from year to year without the question of how much noise a society may impose on its members coming any closer to an answer.
