---
title: The Open Library
---

The town library has stood behind the town hall since 1974, in a low building whose windows reach down to the floor. Anyone entering it at half past seven on a Thursday evening finds light, warmth and two dozen people at the tables – but nobody behind the desk. For the past eighteen months the building has been open four hours longer than it is staffed. You come in through the gate with your card, borrow at a machine and leave the building again without having said a word.

The idea comes from Denmark, where unstaffed opening hours have been common for years, and it answers a problem every smaller municipality knows. Staff are expensive, demand in the evening is hard to predict, and a building that closes at six stands empty precisely when most working people would have time. So instead of cutting the opening hours, the council extended them and replaced supervision with technology: cameras, a door system, an announcement at a quarter to ten.

What was feared above all was vandalism. It has not materialised, at any rate not on a scale that differs from the staffed hours; the assessment after eighteen months lists two damaged chairs and one vanished charging cable. The director puts that down less to the cameras than to the fact that the visitors know one another. People who come in the evening, she says, come regularly.

A different point is viewed more critically. A library, the argument runs, is not a vending machine but a place where people are advised, given recommendations and shown how things work, and anyone who reduces it to shelves and self-service is economising on the very heart of it. The objection carries weight, because precisely those groups that need a public library most urgently – children, older people, newcomers – cope least well with an empty hall.

The director only half disagrees. The unstaffed hours, she says, have not replaced the staffed ones but added to them; not a single advice service has been cut, and the number of events has actually risen. What matters is what the building does with the time it has gained. As long as the open door remains an addition and not the beginning of a savings programme, it is the best thing that has happened to the library in years.
