---
title: The Third Voice in the Courtroom
---

Whoever reads out the charges in a German courtroom is speaking to the court; whoever sits beside the defendant and speaks along under their breath is speaking to a single person. The interpreter, generally sworn in and listed in the summons as no more than an auxiliary, takes her place to the side of the bench, at an angle to everyone involved, as it were, and begins to work the moment the presiding judge opens the session. From then on every sentence is spoken twice, without the second version ever finding its way into the record: what is set down is only what has been said in German.

This matter of course rests on a fiction that procedural law nowhere states outright and could nevertheless not do without – namely the assumption that the rendering is nothing other than the statement itself, merely in different sounds. What the defendant has said counts as what the interpreter has said; his hesitation, his detours, the turn of phrase for which German has no counterpart, are no longer to be found in the files that may later occupy the court of appeal. If a witness contradicts him, he is contradicting a text that a third party has composed.

The difficulties begin beneath the level of the words. A statement that sounds long-winded, rambling or evasive in the original loses those qualities as soon as it is smoothed out, and with them, in some cases, precisely what the court believes it can read as a sign of credibility. Anyone who preserves the long-windedness, on the other hand, runs the risk that it will be put down to the speaker rather than to the language he comes from. Between the two there is no neutral path, only the choice of which mistake to make – and since the interpreter is not a party to the proceedings, she has no one with whom she might discuss that choice.

Her work becomes conspicuous only where it fails. As long as the rendering holds, nobody listens to her; it takes a stumble, a query, a contradiction between two sentences that the same person is supposed to have uttered, to draw attention to the seam where two languages are joined together. That proceedings so concerned with accuracy should leave the most delicate of their steps to a single person whose performance they neither record nor review is one of those oddities one grows used to remarkably quickly in the daily life of the courts.
