---
title: The Stream Returns
---

Anyone crossing Lindenplatz today sees only paving stones between cafés and tram tracks. Beneath them, however, the Mühlenbach flows, enclosed in a concrete pipe laid in the 1960s. At the time, the water was regarded as an obstacle to road traffic and a hygiene problem. Now the stream is to be brought back into daylight after more than half a century.

The project was prompted by several summers with unusually heavy rainfall. The old pipe could not hold the volume of water, so cellars flooded and an underground car park had to be closed twice. Installing a larger pipe would have been technically possible, but would have left little capacity for future rainfall. The city therefore chose a more open, longer-term solution.

Landscape architect Lea Sommer leads the team planning the new course. In her drawings, the stream winds through a shallow green bed with trees, paths and seating steps along its edges. In normal weather, the water occupies only a narrow part of the area. After a storm, by contrast, it is allowed to spread without immediately putting buildings at risk.

As convincing as the concept appears on paper, it is the subject of fierce argument. Business owners fear two years of construction and fewer customers, while residents criticise the loss of parking spaces. Some also doubt that an artificially created channel still has anything to do with nature. Lea replies that even a designed stream can provide a habitat, as long as it is not forced back between straight walls.

Before construction can begin, old utilities have to be moved and contaminated soil investigated. Electricity cables, gas pipes and part of the fibre-optic network run beneath the square, and they are not all fully recorded on any single plan. During the first test drilling, the team even encountered the foundations of a house destroyed in the war. Every surprise changes the schedule and increases the cost.

The question of how much water will remain in the stream is particularly controversial. During dry weeks, it naturally carries barely enough water to be visible. Some of the rainwater from surrounding roofs will therefore be collected in underground basins and released slowly. Critics consider this an artificial performance, while supporters see it as a sensible way to deal with a city that is already entirely designed.

During the planning process, Lea visited similar projects in other European cities. On hot days, temperatures around the open waterways fell noticeably there, and new species of insects and birds appeared. What mattered, however, was whether the banks were planted in varied ways and not neatly cut back every week. A stream treated only as a clean decorative strip performs hardly any ecological function.

The city has now decided to open an initial section two hundred metres long. Sensors will measure how the water level, temperature and biodiversity develop before the second construction phase begins. At the same time, the affected businesses will receive support for deliveries and joint events during the building work. In this way, the administration is trying to show that climate adaptation must not be planned in opposition to everyday life.

There is still no excavator on Lindenplatz, yet the way people perceive the place has already changed. Blue lines on the paving mark where the water runs beneath people's feet, and on guided tours visitors can hear it rushing through an open shaft. What was treated for decades as an invisible technical task is becoming part of the city's history again. When the stream returns in three years, it will therefore not be the same as it was before, but neither will it be merely a new backdrop.
