---
title: The Pact with the Mountain Spirit
---

The city of Arken lay so high in the mountains that snow could remain on its roofs even in May. Nevertheless, lemons, tomatoes and even small peaches grew in long glasshouses behind the city wall. Their warmth came from an underground river that had flowed through the chamber of the mountain spirit Neral for centuries. An ancient pact allowed the inhabitants to use part of this warmth as long as they honoured Neral's name and took only what they truly needed.

Over time, however, the meaning of the word need had changed. At first, the warm water supplied homes and a small hospital, but later workshops, baths and ever larger glasshouses were added. Merchants sold Arken's winter fruit far into the lowlands and described every new order as necessary for the prosperity of the city. Hardly anyone still asked whether the mountain was paying a price as well.

When several tremors shook the walls in summer, the council summoned Sela, the young interpreter of pacts. She was one of the few people who could understand the slow cracking of rock as a language. Deep beneath the city, she heard not anger but an exhausted, irregular breath. Neral no longer slept peacefully because the pipes were now drawing almost all the warmth from his dreams.

The council wanted to reduce the extraction for a few weeks and then continue as usual. Sela warned, however, that a pause would not solve the fundamental problem. The oldest version of the pact stated no fixed share, but only that the city could take what it needed to survive. That very uncertainty had helped both sides for a long time, but had now become the heart of the conflict.

Sela descended into the mountain spirit's chamber with three council members. There, glowing stones formed an enormous face whose eyes opened only slowly. Neral did not demand the complete return of his warmth, for he too remembered the city's first harsh winters. He did, however, want seven years of peace in the lower tunnels and an end to the constantly expanding pipes.

For Arken, immediate agreement would have had serious consequences. Almost a third of the inhabitants now worked in the glasshouses or in the trade in their fruit. Without enough warmth, plants would freeze, businesses would close and families would lose their incomes. Some council members therefore claimed that the mountain spirit's demand threatened the city more than any earthquake.

Sela refused to treat the dispute as a choice between Neral and the people. She had calculations made of which buildings actually needed warmth in winter and how much energy was lost through poorly insulated pipes. They showed that the oldest pipes released almost a quarter of their warmth into empty rock tunnels. In addition, the pools in the private bathhouses consumed more than the hospital and all the schools combined.

At an open meeting, merchants, gardeners and families argued over the results for two days. Eventually, a plan emerged that satisfied no one completely but did not place the burden on only one group. The city would first seal the pipes, close three luxurious baths and limit the cultivation of fruit that required particularly high temperatures. In return, the merchants would invest part of their profits in new reservoirs that could hold summer warmth until winter.

Sela returned to the depths with this proposal. Neral listened for a long time and then placed a hand of stone on the floor of the chamber. New lines appeared in the rock beneath his fingers: the city could receive a fixed basic amount of warmth, but any additional extraction had to be reviewed together every seventh year. The lower tunnels remained closed, and children from Arken were to descend regularly so that the mountain spirit's name would not become an empty formula again.

The new pact changed the city more slowly than its opponents had feared. There were fewer peaches in the first winter, and some merchants had to find other work, but no home remained cold. The repaired pipes saved more warmth than expected, while the reservoirs were filled on sunny days. Most importantly, the tremors stopped, and deep in the mountain Neral's breathing became steady again.

Seven years later, Sela stood once more before the stone chamber. This time she brought no finished demand, but reports on harvests, temperatures and the experiences of the inhabitants. In return, Neral described how the springs and tensions in the rock had changed. Both sides now understood that a fair pact remains alive not because its words apply for ever, but because its consequences are repeatedly examined together.
