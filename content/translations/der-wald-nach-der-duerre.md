---
title: The Forest After the Drought
---

Anyone hiking through the Harz today walks for kilometres between grey, barkless trunks that stand on the slopes like matchsticks. They have long since carried no needles, and where closed forest once swallowed the light, grass now grows between the root plates of fallen trees. For walkers the sight is unsettling; for foresters it is the outcome of a calculation drawn up more than two hundred years ago.

For the German forest, which the nineteenth century declared a symbol of depth and permanence, is in large part not grown nature but a plantation. After the overexploitation of the early modern period, when mining, salt works and charcoal burning had stripped whole stretches of land bare, the areas were replanted with whatever grew fast, straight and saleable: with spruce. It comes from the cool, damp high ground of the uplands and the Alps, but was planted well down into warm lowlands where it had never been native.

As long as the summers reliably brought rain, the calculation worked. The drought years from 2018 onwards put an end to it: three consecutive summers with far below average rainfall dried out the upper layers of soil so thoroughly that the shallow-rooted spruce lost its most important protection. A healthy tree fends off the bark beetle with resin; a tree under water stress can no longer produce that resin. What followed was the greatest calamity in German forestry history — some half a million hectares of damaged land and a timber market that collapsed under the sheer quantity of salvage wood.

The term the forestry authorities have worked with ever since is forest conversion. What is meant is the transition from the even-aged monoculture to mixed, multi-layered stands of beech, oak, fir, maple and lime, which cope better with drought and, because they are susceptible to different things, do not fail together. How this conversion should proceed is disputed. Some rely on planting and on introduced species such as the Douglas fir, others on natural regeneration, that is, on the forest rebuilding itself from the seed of the surviving trees — slower, cheaper and carrying the risk that spruce of all things will return.

Every strategy is made harder by the pattern of ownership. Barely half of the German forest belongs to private owners, and a large part of that is spread over parcels of a few hectares, handed down over generations, whose owners often neither live in the region nor bring any forestry knowledge with them. A conversion whose result only the grandchildren will see is hard to plan against the interests of people who regard the forest as a reserve.

On top of this comes a debate about what the forest is supposed to do at all. It is a supplier of timber, a carbon store, a water filter, a habitat and a place of recreation all at once, and these claims get in each other's way: whoever harvests wood removes stored carbon; whoever leaves the forest to itself forgoes a building material whose production consumes incomparably less energy than that of concrete and steel. Conservation groups therefore demand that larger areas be taken out of use, while the forestry industry points out that an untended stand survives the next catastrophe no better than a managed one.

There will be no robust answer any time soon, because the forest's timescales are not those of politics. What is planted or left unplanted in this decade will only turn out to have been right or wrong around the year 2120 — under climatic conditions nobody today knows precisely. The one certainty left to the foresters is the lesson of the Harz: a forest consisting of a single tree species is exactly as resilient as that species itself.
