---
title: The Island Without Cars
---

When I took a job at a small hotel on a North Sea island in April, I mainly imagined beaches and fresh air. The fact that private cars are not allowed on the island initially sounded to me like an advantage for holidaymakers. Only when I stood at the harbour with two heavy suitcases did I understand that this rule shapes the whole of everyday life. My car stayed behind in a car park on the mainland.

It was almost two kilometres from the ferry terminal to the hotel. Horse-drawn carriages, bicycle taxis and small electric vehicles that could only be used with a special permit were waiting outside the harbour. My new manager collected my luggage with a handcart and walked beside me. As we went through the village, instead of engines I heard only seagulls, bicycle bells and the rolling of our suitcase wheels.

During my first few days, I noticed how carefully deliveries had to be organised. Food, bed linen and drinks arrived on the first ferry in the morning and were distributed among small transport vehicles at the harbour. Because the paths were narrow, no driver could simply stop anywhere and continue later. Anything unloaded in the wrong place often had to be carried by hand halfway across the village.

I soon planned my shopping differently from the way I did at home as well. I could not quickly drive to the nearest supermarket for a forgotten carton of milk, but had to walk for twenty minutes. In a strong wind, the walk back with a full bag felt twice as long. That is why I wrote careful lists and went shopping together with two colleagues.

Thousands of guests arrived every day in summer, and that was when the limits of the system became clear. Many travellers brought large suitcases, pushchairs, bicycles and sometimes even surfboards. Whenever two ferries arrived close together, the luggage piled up at the harbour. Even so, the village remained remarkably quiet because no queue of running engines waited outside the hotels.

Of course, the island cannot function entirely without vehicles. The fire brigade, ambulance service, refuse collection and tradespeople have small electric cars or are allowed to use other vehicles in exceptional cases. Once, a hotel guest had to be taken to the island hospital at night. The quiet ambulance arrived within a few minutes, although I had previously worried that help might come too late on a car-free island.

For the residents, however, the peace and quiet does not mean only freedom. Anyone who is old or unable to walk well depends on transport services and helpful neighbours. Larger pieces of furniture have to be registered weeks in advance so that there is space on the ferry and a transport vehicle. And when the sea is too high because of a storm, neither parcels nor fresh vegetables arrive.

After a few weeks, I bought a second-hand bicycle with a large basket. My journeys took longer than before, but I began to see them as part of the day rather than as wasted time. On the way to work, I almost always met someone I knew. Conversations began by themselves because nobody disappeared behind a car window.

When my three months were over, I took the ferry back and got into my car on the mainland again. At the very first junction, the noise and the smell of exhaust fumes bothered me. I know that the island's model cannot work everywhere. But ever since then, on every short journey I ask myself whether I really need the car or am simply used to it.
