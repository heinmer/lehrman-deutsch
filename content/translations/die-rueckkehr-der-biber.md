---
title: The Return of the Beavers
---

Anyone walking along the bank of the Nidda in spring will find sharpened stumps among the willows that look as though someone had worked on them with a crude tool. In fact they come from an animal that was as good as extinct in Germany for almost two hundred years. The beaver is back, and in numbers that hardly anyone would have reckoned with three decades ago.

In the nineteenth century it was hunted for its fur, its meat and a glandular secretion that was held to be a remedy; what was left were a few hundred animals on the middle reaches of the Elbe. Since the nineteen-seventies the species has been protected, and in several federal states animals were deliberately reintroduced. Today the figure is put at some forty-five thousand beavers, spread across almost the whole country.

For conservationists this is a success of a kind that does not come along often. Where beavers build, the water backs up, the stream loses its pace and spreads out across the land. In the shallow zones that arise, dragonflies, amphibians and fish settle that had long since disappeared from straightened watercourses. What the authorities try to achieve with expensive restoration programmes, the animal does in passing and without an application form.

Not everyone sees it that way. Farmers whose fields run right down to the bank lose ground when a dam pushes the water into them. A farm track undermined from below can give way under a tractor, and a felled fruit tree is, to whoever owns it, no contribution to biodiversity. Since the beaver is strictly protected, neither may the animal be disturbed nor its lodge removed without a permit.

In most federal states there are therefore beaver advisers, who mediate when a dispute arises. They fit pipes into the dams that lower the water level without the animals moving away, protect individual trees with wire collars and check whether a loss can be covered from a compensation fund. An adviser from Upper Bavaria says the conflicts can almost always be resolved as long as people talk to each other early enough. It only becomes difficult, he says, when somebody rings up for the first time once half the field is already under water.

Whether the coexistence works in the long run depends less on the beaver than on the question of how much room people are willing to grant the rivers. A watercourse left with a strip of twenty metres can take a dam without any trouble. Where the plough goes right up to the embankment, conflict is a foregone conclusion.
