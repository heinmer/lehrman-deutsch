---
title: The Night Shift at the Bakery
---

At half past midnight, when the last guests are leaving the pub across the road, Anna Kerner's working day begins. She unlocks the back door, turns on the light and first of all fires up the oven, which needs a good hour to reach the temperature she is counting on. Until then there is time for the dough, which has been standing in the cold room since the evening before, slowly working away in the chill.

Anna is a master baker, thirty-three years old, and took the business over from her former boss two years ago. Anyone who asks her why she puts herself through these hours gets a sober answer: bread takes time, she says, and time simply cannot be hurried. A dough that has been worked for twenty hours tastes different from one that has been helped along with additives, and it sits more lightly in the stomach as well.

That her trade still exists in this form at all is by no means a matter of course. Since the turn of the millennium more than half of all bakeries in Germany have closed; what is left is mostly the big chains, whose branches are supplied with frozen, part-baked loaves. If one day nobody could be found who wanted to carry Anna's business on, it would be the fourth in town to disappear within a few years.

By three o'clock the bakehouse has grown warm. Anna divides the dough, weighs it, shapes it and sets the loaves into their proving baskets without having to look; after fifteen years the movements are so exact that she can talk as she goes without losing the rhythm. What she values about the night is the quiet: no telephone, no suppliers, nobody wanting anything from her.

At six the shop opens, and with the first customers the same question comes up as regularly as ever. Four euros eighty for a loaf, when at the supermarket it costs a third of that. Anna then works through what is in that price: flour from the mill in the next village, electricity, which by now costs more than the flour, and above all working hours that cannot be rationalised away. Most of them hear her out; some do not come back all the same.

Shortly after eleven she locks up behind her. On the way home the town has long been awake while her own day is ending, and for a few streets she feels as though she were travelling in the wrong direction. Whether she would make the same choice again she does not know, she says; but she is not going to sell the shop.
