---
title: The Repair Café
---

On the last Saturday of every month the hall of the community centre turns into a workshop. On the tables lie soldering irons, screwdrivers and sewing machines, and among them the appliances that have refused to do their job: toasters, record players, bicycle lamps. Anyone who brings something along pays nothing, but has to be willing to lend a hand. Because here things are not simply repaired — you are shown how to repair them.

The idea comes from Amsterdam, where the first of these cafés opened a good fifteen years ago. By now there are more than a thousand of them in Germany alone. The turnout has less to do with thrift than one might assume. Many of the visitors could easily afford a new appliance; they come all the same, because they baulk at the thought of throwing away something that a single soldered joint would save.

Herr Baumann, a retired electrical engineer, looks after the table for small appliances. He says that in five years he has hardly seen a device whose fault really had anything to do with the end of its life. Usually, he says, it is a capacitor worth eighty cents, or a cable that has broken at the very same spot as always. What annoys him is not the technology but the way things are built: casings that are glued rather than screwed together can barely be opened without being destroyed in the process.

For some years now the European Union has required that spare parts for certain groups of products remain available for years. Critics regard the rule as a beginning, but no more than that, since it long left out precisely the devices that are replaced most often. Had everything been included from the start, the pressure on manufacturers would have been far greater. For the time being, then, we are left with a patchwork of regulations that hardly anyone can take in.

In the afternoon a student sits in front of a radio from the sixties that once belonged to her grandfather. After two hours the dial lights up again, and out of the loudspeaker comes a hiss in which a voice slowly takes shape. Whether the effort was worth it can hardly be expressed in euros. She says that for the first time she has understood what actually goes on inside that casing, and that this was worth the two hours to her.

At around five o'clock they start clearing up. A list hangs on the door: thirty-one appliances taken in, twenty-two repaired, five fitted with spare parts that still have to be ordered. On the remaining four the helpers gave up, without anyone regarding that as a defeat. That, too, is part of the idea: at least you find out why something is no longer worth it.
