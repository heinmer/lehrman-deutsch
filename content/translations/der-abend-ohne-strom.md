---
title: The Evening Without Electricity
---

Last November, I had already been living in a four-storey building on the edge of town for almost two years. Even so, I knew only a few neighbours by name. When we met on the stairs, we briefly said hello and moved on. Everyone had their own daily routine, and the flat doors were usually closed.

One Tuesday evening, I wanted to quickly cook some soup after work and then finish writing a job application. I had barely put the pot on the hob when the light went out. The hob, the fridge and my computer suddenly went silent as well. At first I thought that a fuse had blown only in my flat.

When I opened the door, however, the whole staircase was dark. From above I heard someone asking whether the electricity was off for everyone else too. Soon phones were shining everywhere, and several neighbours came out of their flats. Herr Scholz from the second floor looked in the fuse box, but he could not find a fault.

We went outside and saw that the street lights and the windows of the other buildings were dark too. Only the cars were driving along the street with bright headlights. One neighbour found a short notice on the town's website: an electricity cable had been damaged during construction work. The repair was expected to take at least three hours.

At first everyone went back into their flats, but after twenty minutes the first people returned to the staircase. Frau Yilmaz from the ground floor brought candles and put them safely in large glasses. Eight-year-old Mika was worried about the fish in his aquarium because the pump was no longer running. Herr Scholz reassured him and explained that the fish would manage for a few hours without the pump.

Then Frau Yilmaz had an idea: we should eat the food from our fridges together before it went bad. Soon there was bread, cheese, salad, yoghurt and a large bowl of fruit in the hallway. Someone fetched a folding table from the cellar, while others brought chairs, plates and cutlery. We could not cook my soup, but nobody had to go hungry.

For the first time, we all sat together and had enough time to talk to one another. I learnt that the quiet woman from the top floor works as a bus driver and gets up at half past four every morning. The young couple next door had moved in only three weeks earlier and hardly knew the town yet. Even Herr Scholz, who often seemed unfriendly at other times, told funny stories from his time in the fire brigade.

I was still worried about my application, because I had to send it by midnight and my phone had almost no power left. The bus driver gave me a fully charged power bank that she always carried for long shifts. I was able to check my documents once more on my phone and send them in time. Without the power cut, I would probably never have asked her for help.

At about ten o'clock, the lights suddenly flickered back on, and music, fridges and televisions could be heard from several flats at once. Everyone clapped, but nobody got up straight away. We stayed at the table for a while longer and ate the last of the cake. Only when Mika wanted to check on his fish did we clear everything away together.

The next weekend we met once again, this time in daylight. We put an emergency box in the cellar with torches, batteries, candles and a small radio. We also created a group on our phones so that we could reach one another quickly if there were any problems. Of course, our doors have not stayed open all the time since that evening, but a brief hello has often turned into a real conversation.
