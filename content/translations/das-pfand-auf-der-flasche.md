---
title: The Deposit on the Bottle
---

Anyone shopping in Germany for the first time is usually taken aback twice: once at the till, where the amount comes out higher than a glance at the price tag suggested, and a second time in front of the machine by the entrance, which swallows empty bottles and prints out a voucher in return. The deposit has seeped so deeply into everyday life that hardly anyone still recognises in it what it was originally meant to be: an instrument of environmental policy.

It was pushed through against considerable resistance. When the federal government imposed a compulsory deposit on single-use drinks packaging in 2003, retailers and the drinks industry warned of runaway bureaucracy, of unreasonable costs and of return machines for which no supermarket would have room. Two decades later the return rate for single-use bottles and cans stands at over ninety-eight percent — a figure most countries without a deposit system come nowhere near.

That the rate is so high, however, has not only to do with consumers' environmental conscience. Twenty-five cents is not a sum many households think about; for others it is a source of income. In the pedestrian zones of the big cities, at railway stations and after festivals, people go from bin to bin with plastic bags, and the fact that their work spares local authorities part of the disposal work is rarely said out loud.

In some places city administrations have had so-called deposit rings fitted to the waste bins, so that the bottles no longer have to be fished out of the rubbish. The gesture is practical and kindly meant, and at the same time an admission is contained in it: the collecting is firmly counted on.

Critics hold against the system that it has missed its actual target. The deposit was meant to protect the reusable bottle, which is washed and refilled up to fifty times; in fact its share has fallen since the scheme began from around two thirds to a good four tenths. Whoever brings back a single-use bottle has not saved a bottle, but merely returned clean, sorted material to the cycle, which is then melted down.

Whether the deposit has in the end achieved more than remarkably clean streets is open to argument. What is not in dispute is that it has shifted the relationship to waste: in this country an empty bottle counts not as rubbish but as an object with residual value — and what has a value does not get thrown away.
