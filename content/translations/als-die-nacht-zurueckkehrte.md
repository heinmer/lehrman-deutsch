---
title: When Night Returned
---

When the last shops in Falkenried close at ten o'clock in autumn, something unusual has been happening for several months: the streetlights grow dimmer. They do not go out completely, but lower their output to a warm base light that leaves paths and junctions only faintly visible. When a pedestrian or bicycle approaches, the next section becomes brighter for a short time. As soon as no one is on the move, darkness returns.

The trial was prompted neither by the energy crisis nor by a particularly ambitious savings programme, but by an inconspicuous study conducted by the local conservation association. Over several summers, its members had counted how many moths died around the lamps near the market square. At the same time, they found that hardly any stars could be seen even outside the centre, although Falkenried had only twelve thousand residents. Their report made clear that artificial light does not simply disappear when it reaches the ground, but is scattered by façades, clouds and wet roads far beyond its intended purpose.

In the town council, the proposal to reduce the lighting initially met with fierce resistance. Business owners feared that a dark town centre might seem unwelcoming, while older residents pointed out that uneven pavements also had to remain visible at night. Others associated brightness with safety as a matter of principle and warned against driving women and young people out of public space. Supporters, in turn, considered it wrong to treat every dark corner automatically as a danger without examining whether more light actually meant less crime.

The mayor therefore proposed not a permanent conversion, but a six-month trial in three streets used in different ways. Motion sensors would gently raise the lamps' brightness, while the lighting at bus stops remained constant throughout the night. Damaged paving stones were also replaced and bushes were cut back at two blind junctions. In this way, the town wanted to prevent problems of path design from later being blamed on darkness.

On the first evening, the administration invited residents on a walk together. The nearly forty participants included sceptical business owners, young people, visually impaired people, police officers and members of the conservation association. After only a few minutes, it became clear how differently darkness is perceived: some found the dimmed light peaceful, while others felt watched because every movement they made triggered a lamp. An older woman also observed that the sudden change between bright and dark affected her sense of direction more severely than a consistently low level of light.

The technicians then changed not the brightness, but the speed of the transition. The light now rose more slowly and remained active for longer, so that no one seemed to be walking through a narrow corridor of brightness. The lamps were also shielded so that they shone only downwards. This unremarkable measure proved particularly effective, because the pavement remained visible while bedroom windows and treetops received considerably less light.

After three months, the first data were available, but they did not permit a simple declaration of success. Electricity consumption in the test streets had fallen by more than half, and substantially fewer dead insects were found at the converted lamps. At the same time, two residents had complained because sensors reacted too late when they left their properties. The police recorded no increase in offences, but rightly pointed out that a short period in a small town hardly allowed for robust conclusions.

What mattered, therefore, was not only what was measured, but also whose experience counted as relevant. The town organised a second walk, this time without representatives of the manufacturer, so that criticism would not be treated as a disruption to a product demonstration. A schoolgirl explained that she would rather choose a slightly longer, evenly lit route home than the shortest route with constantly changing light. A blind participant reminded the group that unobstructed pavements, audible traffic signals and reliable kerbs were more important to him than the intensity of the light.

As a result, the question Falkenried was arguing about gradually changed. It was no longer a matter of whether light was good and darkness bad, but of what light was needed, in what place and at what time. Shop windows, private garden spotlights and illuminated advertising spaces also came under scrutiny, because a municipality has little credibility if it dims public streetlights while façades are lit up all night. Some shopkeepers eventually agreed to switch off their lighting after midnight, provided that the rule applied equally to everyone.

At the end of the trial period, the town council decided to extend the system gradually to more residential streets. However, the same technology was not simply installed everywhere: dangerous junctions, the station forecourt and heavily used cycle paths each received their own designs. The collected complaints remained publicly accessible, and after a year the town would examine again whether people were in fact making fewer journeys on foot. The decision was therefore less spectacular than the promise of a completely dark town, but probably more robust.

On clear nights, a faint band of the Milky Way can now be seen above Falkenried once again. This is no proof that every conflict has been resolved, nor a reason to dismiss the concerns of those who feel unsafe in the dark. Yet the trial has shown that safety, orientation and nature conservation do not inevitably have to be played off against one another. Sometimes a better solution begins by treating light not as a permanent condition to be taken for granted, but as something that can be justified, designed and limited.
