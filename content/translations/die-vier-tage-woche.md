---
title: The Four-Day Week
---

When the management of a mid-sized software company in Leipzig announced that from now on the doors would stay closed on Fridays, many employees at first suspected a pretext for cost-cutting. Instead salaries stayed the same, while working time fell from five days to four. Two years later the company is seen as a trailblazer in its industry: applications now arrive by the hundred, where barely a dozen used to come in, and even former doubters on the board now openly talk of a success.

The idea of the four-day week is older than the current headlines suggest. As early as the 1930s, economists had predicted that technical progress would steadily shorten working hours — a forecast that took a long time to come true. Only when Iceland, between 2015 and 2019, let over two thousand five hundred employees work shorter hours on full pay in a broadly based trial overseen by the city administration of Reykjavík, without productivity falling, did the idea become a topic taken seriously in politics. Similar pilot projects followed in Britain, Spain and, with a considerable delay, in Germany too.

Supporters point to studies according to which concentration improves and sick-leave rates fall as soon as an extra free day is available for recovery. In the British pilot study, involving more than sixty companies from finance, charity work and technology, almost ninety percent of participants said they wanted to keep the model once the trial had ended. The shorter working day also seems to pay off from a business point of view: someone who knows that the working day ends at four o'clock postpones meetings less often, does without casual interruptions and, as several personnel departments have observed, concentrates on what matters.

Critics counter that the model mainly proves itself in office jobs, where working time and output are not directly linked. In care work, retail or catering, where customer traffic and opening hours set the pace, a day can hardly simply be cut without either hiring more staff or compressing the remaining time accordingly. That, however, produces exactly the strain the model is meant to reduce. Unions also warn that employers could use the shortened week as a bargaining chip to demand greater flexibility or availability outside agreed hours in return.

A labour market researcher at the University of Bochum, who has scientifically accompanied several German pilot companies, therefore advises against sweeping conclusions. What matters, she says, is not the number of working days as such, but whether an organisation actually manages to simplify its processes, rather than merely doing the same work in less time. If that change is neglected, the supposed relief threatens to turn into extra pressure that employees would ultimately have to bear themselves.

Whether the four-day week establishes itself as a widespread model or is remembered as a marginal phenomenon confined to a few industries cannot yet be conclusively judged today. What is certain is only that the question of how much working time is necessary for a good life is unlikely to lose any urgency in an ageing society with a growing shortage of skilled workers.
